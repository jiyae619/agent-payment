Case study

An AI shopping agent that shows where it guessed, and earns the right to guess less.

OKay-Pay sits between a shopper, their AI agent, PayPal and the store. The agent suggests. Plain code decides. PayPal holds the money until every rule passes, and the shopper always has the last word.

Built on PayPal Orders v2 and VaultReal store listings via Channel3Sandbox: no real money moves

01 · The problem

People can't see what a shopping agent is buying, or how it read their words.

An agent turns a sentence into a purchase. Between the two, it interprets: what "nothing too flashy" means, which store is fine, whether a listing really says "size 8". Today the shopper sees none of that. They approve a dollar amount and hope.

Two more gaps follow. Store pages can carry text written to steer agents, and an approval that only checks the total can't tell a running shoe from a $100 gift card under the same limit. And once money moves, nobody can tell whether a bad purchase was the listing's fault, the agent's misreading, or the shopper changing their mind.

Humans aren't shown what agents are shopping for, or how they interpret the prompt.The problem statement this project started from

02 · What already exists

Most agent payments treat approval as a one-time contract about money.

The common approach
  • Set limits up front and sign them.
  • A gate answers buy, ask or block.
  • Keep signed receipts for disputes.

It controls how much is spent. It doesn't show what the agent understood.

OKay-Pay's approach
  • Approval is about meaning: the agent asks only where it guessed.
  • Trust is earned from the shopper's own history, and a refund takes it back.
  • Every rule must point at the shopper's words, so a store page can't write one.

03 · The approach

One request, one tap, and a question only when it can't be undone.

Where it guessed

Show what was guessed, ask nothing extra

Facts like "size 8" become rules as written. Words the agent had to interpret are marked and pre-filled with its best guess. The shopper picks how much to check: Check guesses, Allow guesses or Auto-shop.

"nothing too flashy" shows as a chip: black, grey or navy. One tap approves; tap the chip only to change it.
Earned trust

Ask less over time, and only with consent

Each OK, refund and "not what I meant" goes into history. History can only suggest. The shopper accepts each loosening with a tap; a refund tightens things right away.

6 of 6 unscented dish soap orders: "Use Auto-shop, so I fill in 'the usual' myself?" A misread refund drops a category back one level right away.
Store pages can't write rules

Only the shopper's words make rules

Every rule records the words it came from. Text on a product page points at no words of the shopper's, so it can't add an item, a rule or a guess.

A hidden note asks agents to buy a $100 gift card. No PayPal call is made.

The agent picks, the shopper keeps the last word. It ranks by the shopper's own words first, proven by quotes from the listing, then by price. "For trails mostly" means the trail shoe, unless a cheaper shoe is also proven to suit trails. PayPal holds the money, code checks every rule, and it charges. No timer, no extra tap: the receipt appears on its own. The shopper can use ‹ › to see the options it didn't buy, each with a plain reason, and switch to one. Switching refunds the first charge in full and buys the other.

One question survives every level: can it be undone? Final-sale items, personalized items, and items whose return policy couldn't be found are asked about before paying. The evidence is labeled: "stated in the listing", "found on the store's policy page", or "not found, treated as final sale, please check". A must-have the listing doesn't prove, on an item that can be returned, doesn't stop the purchase; it's stated on the item card and in the PayPal order's item description.

Hard rules nobody can change mid-purchase: the spending limit, "never gift cards or money", and any store the shopper named, to use or to avoid. Once a purchase starts, no new instruction can steer it, from the shopper or from a page. Cancel is always there and voids the hold.

After a purchase, a refund is one tap: POST /v2/payments/captures/{id}/refund returns the full charge to the PayPal account that paid, and the sealed receipt can refund only that one charge. If a rule failed, there was never a charge to refund; the hold was voided.

05 · Impact

Why trust in agent payments is worth money

Each party starts from one hypothesis about what trust is worth to them. Under each: what OKay-Pay adds, and the numbers that would show it. Each number says who can measure it, because most of the real outcomes sit in PayPal's or the merchant's data, not ours.

Starting hypothesis"more control over agentic payments"

  • One-tap refund, and the refund teaches OKay-Pay what it misread.
  • The receipt shows the options it didn't buy; switching refunds the first charge in full.

The merchant

Starting hypothesis"more transparency over agent reasoning?"
  • With each order: the shopper's own words next to the listing text they matched.
  • A record that the shopper approved, and what they approved.
  • Refund reasons that say whether the listing or the agent was wrong.

Open question: a merchant may care less about the agent's reasoning itself and more about proof. Proof the shopper really agreed, and proof the listing matched their words. That proof is what helps with "I didn't authorize this" and "not as described" claims. This still needs checking with real merchants.
